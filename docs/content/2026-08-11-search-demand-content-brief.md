## Executive decision

The next two editorial priorities are:

1. **Somatic Therapy vs. Talk Therapy: A Clear, Careful Comparison**
2. **What Happens During a First Therapy Consultation?**

The somatic comparison comes first. Healing Tides already has a small but real search foothold for
Nora's Somatic Series, national search interest has risen materially, and many current results reduce
the subject to an unhelpful body-versus-mind binary. Nora can add what generic explainers cannot:
the exact language she uses, examples of real invitations, how consent works, where she stays verbal,
and where her training or scope ends.

The consultation article comes second. Search interest is also rising, but its job must stay distinct
from the live first-call worksheet. The article explains the sequence, purpose, variability, and limits
of a preliminary conversation. The worksheet owns the question-selection task.

This is not evidence for a publishing sprint. It is evidence for two carefully owned pages.

## Page one — somatic therapy versus talk therapy

**Search job:** Help a reader understand what the labels may mean, where they overlap, and what to
ask before consenting to a particular way of working.  
**Not its job:** Recommend a modality, diagnose from preferences, prove a broad category effective,
or rank for Minnesota practitioner-service intent.  
**Distinctive contribution:** Nora's own definition, two real examples, consent language, training,
adaptation, and referral boundary.  
**Conversion path:** Somatic Series → comparison → first-call worksheet or Get Matched.  
**Primary source boundary:** NIMH supports psychotherapy as a broad family that may integrate
multiple elements. Research claims about body-oriented work stay tied to the named method and study.

Questions the page must answer plainly:

- Is “somatic therapy” separate from talk therapy?
- Can a therapist combine conversation and body awareness?
- Does somatic therapy always involve touch?
- What should a reader ask about training, consent, and a typical session?
- What can the research support, and what remains uncertain?

## Page two — first therapy consultation

**Search job:** Reduce uncertainty about the possible before, opening, middle, closing, and after
stages of a preliminary therapy conversation.  
**Not its job:** Promise a free call, a fixed duration, a universal confidentiality rule, or a way to
determine fit in minutes.  
**Distinctive contribution:** Nora's real process, what she listens for, what she does not require a
person to disclose, how she handles uncertainty, and when she refers elsewhere.  
**Conversion path:** consultation guide → first-call worksheet → Get Matched.  
**Primary source boundary:** NIMH supports a preliminary conversation as one possible way to learn
how treatment may proceed and whether the person feels comfortable; practice-specific details must
come from the practice.

Questions the page must answer plainly:

- Is a consultation the same thing as an intake or first therapy session?
- What may happen before, during, and after the conversation?
- What might a practitioner ask?
- What can a person ask or decline to explain?
- What are reasonable next steps if they are still unsure?

## Editorial and release gates

1. Record one 35–45 minute interview with Nora using the existing review packet.
2. Use Nora's words only for facts about her process, judgment, training, or practice.
3. Keep every health claim tied to a primary or official source and its actual scope.
4. Show the editorial method, source-check date, real author/reviewer, and correction path.
5. Publish through Sanity; do not hardcode clinician-authored articles into app routes.
6. Give each article a self-canonical, accurate Article schema, one H1, and an `/about` author link.
7. Have Nora approve the rendered final page after the final substantive edit.
8. Request indexing, then measure page-level and query-level GSC data at 14, 30, 60, and 90 days.
