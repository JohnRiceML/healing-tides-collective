## Opening

If talking has helped you understand a pattern but has not always changed how the experience feels, the phrase *somatic therapy* may catch your attention.

The labels can be useful, but they are not opposites. Many practitioners combine conversation, reflection, practical skills, and attention to bodily experience. A better question than “Which one is better?” is: “What does this practitioner actually do, and is that way of working understandable and acceptable to me?”

## Why the comparison gets confusing

*Talk therapy* is an umbrella term, not one technique. Cognitive, psychodynamic, relational, and other approaches can feel very different from one another.

*Somatic therapy* is also an umbrella term. Depending on the practitioner, it may describe licensed psychotherapy that includes body awareness, a named body-oriented method, movement-based work, or a nonclinical wellness practice.

The label alone does not tell you:

- What will happen in a session
- Whether touch is ever used
- What training the practitioner has
- Whether the service is psychotherapy
- What evidence exists for the specific method and concern
- Whether the practitioner’s style will suit you

## Two currents, a shared shoreline

### Talk-centered attention

The work may emphasize thoughts, emotions, meaning, behavior, relationships, reflection, or structured skills. Talking does not necessarily ignore the body; a talk-centered practitioner may still notice tension, breathing, fatigue, or the physical experience of emotion.

### Shared ground

Both kinds of work may include a therapeutic relationship, consent, pacing, goals, reflection, mindfulness, practical skills, and feedback. Many practitioners integrate elements rather than staying inside one label.

### Body-attuned attention

A practitioner may invite attention to sensation, posture, breath, movement, or a wish to slow down. These invitations can happen alongside ordinary conversation. Touch is not inherent to somatic work, and the term should never make its use ambiguous.

## What the research can and cannot say

Research must be described at the level of a specific method and concern—not the broad marketing term *somatic therapy*.

A 2021 scoping review of Somatic Experiencing reported preliminary positive findings, while also finding mixed overall study quality and calling for more unbiased randomized research. A broader 2021 review of body psychotherapy found promising results alongside a need for larger, better-defined studies. These findings do not establish every service described as somatic as effective for every person or concern.

> Interest in body-oriented therapy is growing, but “promising” is not the same as “proven.” Personal preference does not replace appropriate training, consent, or clinical judgment.

Do not make Polyvagal Theory the center of this page. If it is mentioned, link to the existing article with its visible scientific caveat.

## Do you have to choose one?

Not always. Some practitioners integrate body awareness into otherwise talk-centered work. Other practitioners stay primarily with conversation. The useful question is not which label wins; it is how the practitioner works and whether you can understand, question, accept, or decline what they propose.

## A reflection, not a recommendation

**When something is hard, what feels easiest to notice first?**

- The thoughts, story, or meaning
- Sensations, energy, posture, or an urge to move
- Both
- I am not sure

The response is a conversation starter—not a test, diagnosis, or treatment recommendation.

Questions it can help you ask:

- What do you mean by “somatic”?
- What might happen during a typical session?
- Is touch ever used, and how is consent handled?
- Can I decline an exercise or ask to stay with conversation?
- What training do you have in the specific approach you name?
- When would you recommend a different kind of care?

## Automatic rejection conditions

- “The body stores trauma,” “releases trauma,” or “resets/recalibrates the nervous system” presented literally
- “Bottom-up” and “top-down” presented as two discrete biological systems
- A claim that talk therapy ignores the body or somatic work is faster, deeper, better, or universally best for trauma
- Somatic Experiencing® used as a synonym for all somatic therapy
- Any diagnosis, outcome promise, universal exercise, or unverified Nora practice/training claim
- A quiz score or recommendation of one therapy type
