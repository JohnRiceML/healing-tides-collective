# What happens on a first call with a therapist

You have a name, you have decided to dial, and then the question arrives: what will they *ask* me?

Part of the answer is written down. Minnesota rules and statutes set out what has to be disclosed about money, what has to be explained before you consent, when confidentiality can be broken without you, and what a later assessment has to cover. What is *not* written down anywhere is the call itself.

⚠ CONFIRM: no Minnesota law was located requiring a free initial phone consultation, or setting any length or required content for a first call. The familiar "free 15 minutes" appears to be professional convention, not regulation.

So this piece is organized around the logistics — fit, money, coverage, scheduling — because those are the parts Minnesota actually regulates. The therapy and the diagnostic assessment are separate steps, later, with their own rules.

**One scope note, because it governs half of what follows.** Minnesota Rules Chapter 2150 is the Board of Behavioral Health and Therapy's rule chapter. It binds licensed professional counselors and licensed professional clinical counselors (LPC/LPCC) — not social workers, psychologists, psychiatrists, marriage and family therapists, or psychiatric nurses, who each answer to a different board under a different rule set. Where something below is marked **(LPC/LPCC)**, that is whose conduct the rule governs. It is not a universal patient right, and it is not a promise about the clinician you happen to be calling. ⚠ CONFIRM: equivalent provisions for the other five license families were not located or compared. Nora — flag anywhere a reader could reasonably carry an LPC/LPCC rule into an appointment with an LICSW.

## What the call is for

**Money — before services, not after.** A counselor "shall disclose the fees for professional services to a client before providing the services," and the applicable fee schedule is a mandatory informed-consent item alongside the limits to the client's privacy — consent that may be given orally or in writing (**LPC/LPCC**; [Minn. R. 2150.7580 subp. 1](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7580/) and [2150.7525](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7525/)). For social workers, informed consent must be in language the client can understand — with an interpreter if needed — and must cover costs and terms of payment, reasonable alternatives, and the right to refuse or withdraw consent ([Minn. Stat. 148E.230](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/148E.230)). Asking what it costs is not rude; it is the conversation the rules expect.

If you are uninsured, or insured and choosing not to use it, federal rules add a written estimate. CMS states that if you are not using insurance, a provider must give you a **good faith estimate** of expected charges "if you request one or schedule services at least 3 business days in advance"; you can ask for one before scheduling anything and it is due within 3 business days; and if you schedule 0–2 business days out you are not entitled to one ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medical-bill-rights/help/guides/good-faith-estimate)). If a later bill exceeds the estimate by at least $400, a patient-provider dispute resolution process is available, and while it is pending the provider may not move the bill into collections or threaten to, must pause collections already started, and may not charge late fees on the disputed amount ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/nosurprises/providers-payment-resolution-with-patients)).

**Coverage.** The benefits check at first contact is expressly allowed: when someone first makes contact about counseling, the provider may — **with the potential client's oral consent** — contact third parties to determine payment or benefits information and arrange precertification, before there is a client at all (**LPC/LPCC**; [Minn. R. 2150.7515 subp. 13](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7515/)). On the insurance side: outpatient mental health cost-sharing and visit limits cannot be more restrictive than outpatient medical ([Minn. Stat. 62Q.47](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62Q.47)), a plan cannot apply a stricter "medically necessary" standard to mental health than the statutory one ([Minn. Stat. 62Q.53](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62Q.53)), and MDH says parity reaches prior-authorization requirements, not only dollars and visit counts ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/facilities/insurance/managedcare/faq/parity.html)).

**Who you are talking to.** Six license categories qualify as a Minnesota "mental health professional": psychiatric APRN/CNS, LICSW, licensed psychologist, board-certified or board-eligible psychiatrist, LMFT, and LPCC ([Minn. Stat. 245I.04](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/245I.04)). If the person you are calling is an LPC or LPCC, their board's rules also require that at the initial meeting they make accurate information about their qualifications available — **including whether they are still completing postlicensure supervised practice** ([Minn. R. 2150.7570 subp. 2](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7570/)); that on request they give a nontechnical explanation of the procedures used, and that a clients' bill of rights be displayed or available as a handout ([2150.7550](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7550/)); that either party may end the relationship ([2150.7530 subp. 1](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7530/)); and that no one may pay or accept payment for referring you ([2150.7580 subp. 5](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7580/)). Those are counselor obligations under one board's chapter — if you are seeing one of the other five license types, ask that clinician what their board requires rather than assuming this list travels.

## The question about safety

Two things are true here, and they are not the same thing.

The first is a narrow exception to confidentiality. For LPCs and LPCCs, private information may be disclosed without the client's consent "when disclosure is necessary to protect against a clear and substantial risk of imminent serious harm being inflicted by the client on the client or another individual, or as otherwise provided by law or court order" — and then only to law enforcement, the potential victim, the client's family, or appropriate third parties in a position to prevent or avert the harm (**LPC/LPCC**; [Minn. R. 2150.7515 subp. 2](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7515/)).

The second is the separate statutory "duty to warn," which is narrower and differs by license. For **psychologists** it arises only on a specific, serious threat of physical violence against a specific, clearly identified or identifiable potential victim — and that section **expressly does not apply to a threat to commit suicide or other threats by a client to harm the client** ([Minn. Stat. 148.975](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/148.975), subds. 2 and 6). The **LPC/LPCC** provision is worded differently and **does** reach a specific, serious threat of physical violence to self ([Minn. Stat. 148B.593](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/148B.593)). That is a real difference depending on whom you see.

⚠ CONFIRM: no duty-to-warn provision was located for Minnesota social workers (LICSW/LGSW), and it is unchecked whether the psychologists' suicide exclusion has any counterpart for LMFTs or psychiatric APRNs. Do not generalize across license families. Nora — this paragraph needs your read.

One narrow point about being dropped as a client, and it is narrower than it first sounds. In the psychologists' chapter, nothing in the duty-to-warn subdivision "shall be construed to authorize a licensee to terminate treatment of a client as a direct result of a client's violent behavior or threat of physical violence unless the client is referred to another practitioner or appropriate health care facility" ([Minn. Stat. 148.975](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/148.975), subd. 5). Read the limits with it: that is the Board of Psychology's chapter, so it does not speak for the other license families; the trigger is violent behavior or a threat of physical violence; and subd. 6 says the section does not apply to a threat to commit suicide or other threats by a client to harm the client. If what you are afraid of is saying out loud that you have been thinking about suicide, this is not a provision that protects you, and no source located here says you cannot be referred elsewhere. ⚠ CONFIRM: no comparable continuity-of-care-on-termination provision was located for the other license families. Nora — if the honest answer is "ask the clinician directly what happens next," say that instead.

Therapists are also mandated reporters. Suspected child maltreatment, including maltreatment that occurred within the preceding three years, must be reported immediately ([Minn. Stat. 260E.06](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/260E.06)), as must suspected vulnerable-adult maltreatment, to the state's common entry point ([Minn. Stat. 626.557](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/626.557)). If you are seeing a social worker, they must inform you at the beginning of the professional relationship — and again during it as needed — of the circumstances under which they may be required to disclose your information without your consent ([Minn. Stat. 148E.230](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/148E.230)). No source located here requires that this be said out loud rather than handed to you in the intake paperwork; the counselors' rule expressly allows informed consent to be oral or written ([Minn. R. 2150.7525](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7525/)). If it was only in the forms, you can still ask for it in plain words.

## After the call: the assessment is a separate thing

⚠ CONFIRM — SCOPE: the rules below come from [Minn. Stat. 245I.10](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/245I.10), which binds "license holders" — DHS-licensed programs and certain public-program providers. It was **not** verified that a solo private practice seeing a commercially insured or self-pay client is bound by its content list, session caps, or outcome rule. This section is framed as *what Minnesota requires of licensed mental health programs*. Confirm that framing rather than shipping it as a hedge.

⚠ re-check: 245I.10 is mid-amendment (2026 Regular Session), so the lists and session caps below may not be the operative language on the publish date.

The **assessment**, not the therapy, is the gatekeeping step — a license holder must use a client's diagnostic assessment *or* crisis assessment to determine eligibility for mental health services. It is not a phone process: a brief assessment requires a face-to-face interview plus a written evaluation, covering five things — age; symptoms and reason for referral; prior mental health treatment; cultural influences; and a mental status exam. Three details are worth knowing before you go, all from [245I.10](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/245I.10):

- **Therapy can begin before the assessment is finished** — up to three psychotherapy, family psychotherapy, or family psychoeducation sessions. A completed brief assessment then supports up to ten sessions in 12 months without prior authorization.
- **The logistics questions are the assessment, not small talk.** A standard assessment asks about living situation and household, the status of basic needs, education and employment, current medications, and immediate risks to health and safety. Adults 18 and over must be screened for substance use disorder using CAGE-AID or DSM criteria — the alcohol and drug questions are mandated, not prying.
- **Trauma history can wait.** If the assessor cannot gather it without retraumatizing you or harming your willingness to engage in treatment, the law directs them to note which topics need further assessment later.

You are meant to be asked what you want — the assessor must consult you and your family about which services you prefer — and you may not have to retell everything, since prior providers' information and earlier assessments can be used if the source is documented. A completed assessment has exactly two possible endings: a diagnosis with recommended services, **or** a written finding that you do not meet the criteria for a mental health disorder ([245I.10](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/245I.10)). The statute treats both as outcomes of the process. Not meeting the criteria is a documented finding, not a failed appointment — it is also not the same as being told nothing is wrong, and it does not settle what you do next.

## Records, other people, video, teenagers

Before an LPC or LPCC contacts your partner, parent, school, or previous provider for collateral information, they must obtain your consent unless consent is not required by law or court order; a minor client must be told **at the beginning** of the relationship that the law limits their privacy, though that requirement is waived where the minor cannot reasonably be expected to understand the statement; and where services are provided to dual clients — couples or family work — each client must be told at the outset about any limits to their individual privacy (**LPC/LPCC**; [Minn. R. 2150.7515 subps. 3, 4, 5](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/2150.7515/)).

Releasing records elsewhere requires a signed, dated consent, valid **one year** by default — with an exception for providers within related health care entities who are currently involved in your treatment ([Minn. Stat. 144.293](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/144.293)). On written request, a provider has 30 calendar days to give you your own records, in terms and language you can reasonably be expected to understand ([Minn. Stat. 144.292](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/144.292)). And Minnesota's minor-consent statute covers pregnancy-related care, sexually transmitted disease, and alcohol and other drug abuse — it does **not** list general outpatient mental health treatment ([Minn. Stat. 144.343](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/144.343)).

**When someone else asks about you,** three different rules sit inside one section, and they do not all point the same way ([Minn. Stat. 144.294](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/144.294)):

- On the **written request of a spouse, parent, child, or sibling**, the provider's duty is to ask *you* whether you want to authorize that person to receive information about your current and proposed treatment — not to disclose (subd. 1).
- Separately, a provider **may** release a limited set of information — diagnosis, admission to or discharge from treatment, medication names and dosages, side effects, consequences of not taking the medication, and a summary of the discharge plan — to a family member or other person who asks **in writing**, but only where that person lives with you, cares for you, or is directly involved in monitoring your treatment; that involvement is verified and documented; you are told in writing beforehand who asked, why, and what was requested; and you agree, do not object, or are unable to decide. The provider must not disclose if it would be detrimental to your physical or mental health or likely to lead to harm (subd. 3).
- And one disclosure is **mandatory**, not optional: a provider **must** disclose mental health records to a law enforcement agency that gives your name and states both that you are currently involved in a mental health crisis the agency has responded to, and that disclosure is necessary to protect your health or safety or another person's. It is limited to the minimum necessary for a safe response, and the agency must inform you afterward that the records were obtained (subd. 2).

⚠ re-check: 144.294 subd. 2 was amended in the 2026 Regular Session (Ch. 121, Art. 8, Sec. 2). Read the session law's effective date and the amended text before publishing this paragraph.

If the appointment is remote: mental health professionals, practitioners, and clinical trainees are covered telehealth providers; email is **not** telehealth; and audio-only counts only until **July 1, 2027**, for a scheduled appointment where the standard of care can be met that way, or an enrollee-initiated crisis contact ([Minn. Stat. 62A.673](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). ⚠ re-check: that sunset date changes if the legislature extends it — verify by Q2 2027.

## If nobody has capacity

Minnesota sets no legal maximum wait for a therapy appointment: networks must be adequate enough that services come "without unreasonable delay," and wait time is only a criterion MDH's commissioner *may* use in judging that ([Minn. Stat. 62K.10](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62K.10)). The one hard number is geographic — the lesser of 30 miles or 30 minutes to the nearest mental health provider ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/facilities/insurance/managedcare/faq/referral.html)).

If access itself is the problem, MDH takes complaints specifically about limited access to providers, free and with no login to file online ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/facilities/insurance/managedcare/complaint/investigate.html)); public-program enrollees start with the DHS Ombudsman for Public Managed Health Care Programs, 651-431-2660 ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/facilities/insurance/managedcare/faq/parity.html)). Complaints about an individual therapist go to their board instead — Behavioral Health and Therapy 651-201-2756, Social Work 612-617-2100, Psychology 612-617-2230 ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/facilities/insurance/managedcare/complaint/qoc.html)). [FastTrackerMN.org](https://fasttrackermn.org/) is a statewide tool for finding mental health and substance use services, available at no cost with no ads; its homepage describes its listings as showing "the most recent available info" rather than live availability. ⚠ re-check: its own homepage says it is seeking long-term funding, so confirm the site is still live before each republish.

## If you are in distress right now

Healing Tides Collective is a directory, not a crisis service. If you need support today, **call or text 988**. In Minnesota, 988 is staffed by trained specialists at local Minnesota Lifeline centers and is free, confidential, and available 24/7. It is not suicide-only — MDH lists a tough day, feeling overwhelmed, money worries, relationships, grief, identity, anger, and substance use concerns as reasons to reach out. No insurance, no ID, no personal disclosures required. Spanish by pressing 2, interpreters for other languages, ASL by videophone or the "ASL Now" button on 988lifeline.org, veterans line by pressing 1. MDH states 988 contacts 911 only when a life is in immediate danger, and that fewer than 2% of Lifeline calls require a connection to emergency services ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/suicide/988/questions.html)). You can also text **MN** to **741741** for Minnesota's 24/7 Crisis Text Line ([FastTrackerMN](https://fasttrackermn.org/)).
