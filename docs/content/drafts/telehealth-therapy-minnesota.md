If you are looking for a therapist in Minnesota and you would rather meet by video, or by phone, the practical question is usually not whether telehealth "works." It is whether your insurance treats it like a real appointment, and whether it will still do so next year.

Minnesota answers the first part in statute — with conditions. The second part has a date attached.

## The short version, as of July 29, 2026

For most people with commercial insurance sold, issued, or renewed in Minnesota, telehealth therapy must be covered in the same manner as any other covered benefit, and the therapist must be paid at the same rate as for an in-person visit. Those rules do not override your plan's ordinary limits: the statute expressly does not force a carrier to cover something that is not medically necessary or not covered by your plan, and it lets carriers keep reasonable medical management. Phone-only sessions currently count as telehealth, but that specific piece of the definition expires **July 1, 2027**. People on Medical Assistance or MinnesotaCare are covered by a different statute, with its own parity rule and its own 2027 date. And several kinds of coverage sit outside Minnesota's mandate entirely.

## If you have commercial insurance

The governing law is [Minn. Stat. § 62A.673](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673), which the legislature named the Minnesota Telehealth Act. It does two distinct things that are easy to blur together.

**Coverage parity.** A health plan sold, issued, or renewed by a health carrier in Minnesota "must (1) cover benefits delivered through telehealth in the same manner as any other benefits covered under the health plan, and (2) comply with this section" ([§ 62A.673, subd. 3(a)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)).

**Payment parity.** Separately, the carrier "must reimburse the health care provider for services delivered through telehealth on the same basis and at the same rate as the health carrier would apply to those services if the services had been delivered by the health care provider through in-person contact" ([subd. 5(a)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). Note that the same subdivision leaves room for negotiated arrangements: nothing in it "prohibits a health carrier and health care provider from entering into a contract that includes a value-based reimbursement arrangement" that may include telehealth ([subd. 5(d)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)).

Therapy is squarely inside this. The statute's definition of "health care provider" expressly names "a mental health professional under section 245I.04, subdivision 2; a mental health practitioner under section 245I.04, subdivision 4; a clinical trainee under section 245I.04, subdivision 6," along with a treatment coordinator, an alcohol and drug counselor, and a recovery peer under section 245G.11 ([subd. 2(c)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)).

Four narrower provisions do specific work, and they are the ones worth reading closely:

- **No telehealth surcharge.** A carrier may require a deductible, copay, or coinsurance for a telehealth service, "provided that the deductible, co-payment, or coinsurance payment is not in addition to, and does not exceed, the deductible, co-payment, or coinsurance applicable for the same service provided through in-person contact" ([subd. 3(d)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)).
- **Geography is not a gate, but your network still is.** The provision reads in full: "Coverage for services delivered through telehealth must not be limited on the basis of geography, location, or distance for travel subject to the health care provider network available to the enrollee through the enrollee's health plan" ([subd. 3(b)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). Minnesota has no rural-only rule and no originating-site rule. It also does not give you a right to see any Minnesota telehealth therapist regardless of location — the geography-neutrality operates inside the provider network your plan makes available to you.
- **No separate telehealth-only network.** A carrier "must not create a separate provider network to deliver services through telehealth that does not include network providers who provide in-person care to patients for the same service or require an enrollee to use a specific provider within the network to receive services through telehealth" ([subd. 3(c)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). An in-network therapist who also sees people in the office counts.
- **No app fee.** "A health carrier or health care provider shall not require an enrollee to pay a fee to download a specific communication technology or application" ([subd. 4(d)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)).

Prior authorization follows the same logic: it "may be required for health care services delivered through telehealth only if prior authorization is required before the delivery of the same service through in-person contact" ([subd. 4(b)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). Utilization review is allowed, "provided the utilization review is conducted in the same manner and uses the same clinical review criteria as a utilization review for the same services delivered through in-person contact" ([subd. 4(c)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)).

## Where the mandate stops

Two different kinds of limit matter here: limits the statute writes into itself, and coverage that sits outside the statute altogether.

**Limits inside the statute.** Subdivision 3(e) is the paragraph a reader in a coverage dispute needs most. "Nothing in this section: (1) requires a health carrier to provide coverage for services that are not medically necessary or are not covered under the enrollee's health plan; or (2) prohibits a health carrier from: (i) establishing criteria that a health care provider must meet to demonstrate the safety or efficacy of delivering a particular service through telehealth for which the health carrier does not already reimburse other health care providers for delivering the service through telehealth; (ii) establishing reasonable medical management techniques, provided the criteria or techniques are not unduly burdensome or unreasonable for the particular service; or (iii) requiring documentation or billing practices designed to protect the health carrier or patient from fraudulent claims, provided the practices are not unduly burdensome or unreasonable for the particular service" ([subd. 3(e)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). The statute also does not force telehealth on anyone: nothing in it "requires the use of telehealth when a health care provider determines that the delivery of a health care service through telehealth is not appropriate or when an enrollee chooses not to receive a health care service through telehealth" ([subd. 3(f)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)).

So the accurate formulation is: must cover, on the same terms and at the same rate — subject to medical necessity, plan coverage, and reasonable medical management.

**Coverage outside the statute.**

- **Medical Assistance and MinnesotaCare are excluded.** "This section does not apply to coverage provided to state public health care program enrollees under chapter 256B or 256L" ([subd. 8](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). Those enrollees are covered by a separate statute, below.
- **Self-insured employer plans are generally outside it.** The mandate binds "health carriers," a closed list: insurers licensed under chapter 60A, nonprofit health service plan corporations under chapter 62C, HMOs under chapter 62D, fraternal benefit societies under chapter 64B, and joint self-insurance employee health plans under chapter 62H ([Minn. Stat. § 62A.011, subd. 2](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.011)). A single employer's self-funded ERISA plan is not on that list.
- **Medicare supplement and Medicare Advantage-type products are excluded** from the definition of "health plan," which does not include coverage "issued as a supplement to Medicare ... or those policies, contracts, or certificates governed by section 1833 or 1876, section 1851, et seq.; or section 1860D-1, et seq., of title XVIII of the federal Social Security Act" ([§ 62A.011, subd. 3(10)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.011)). Section 1851 et seq. is Medicare Part C, so Minnesota's parity rules do not reach these products.
- **The provider has to be Minnesota-licensed or registered.** "Health care provider" means a professional "who is licensed or registered by the state to perform health care services within the provider's scope of practice and in accordance with state law" ([§ 62A.673, subd. 2(c)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). An out-of-state therapist without Minnesota licensure is outside this framework.

## The phone-call question, and its expiration date

Audio-only matters most where video is hardest to use. Minnesota's own review is blunt that telehealth access is uneven: "telehealth does expand access to health care, but access to telehealth itself is not equitable, particularly for people with limited digital access or digital literacy" ([MDH telehealth final report, September 16, 2024](https://www.health.state.mn.us/data/economics/telehealth/docs/telefinalreport24.pdf)). DHS made the same point to legislators, naming people who "live in rural areas, have limited mobility or lack access to transportation," and "people who don't have the technology required for audiovisual telehealth" ([DHS 2025 session priority sheet, 03/2025](https://mn.gov/dhs/assets/2025-03-audio-only-telehealth-hc-54_tcm1053-669211.pdf)).

**Commercial plans.** Audio-only currently counts as telehealth, on a clock. The statute reads: "Until July 1, 2027, telehealth also includes audio-only communication between a health care provider and a patient if the communication is a scheduled appointment and the standard of care for that particular service can be met through the use of audio-only communication or if, for substance use disorder treatment services and mental health care services delivered through telehealth by means of audio-only communication, the communication was initiated by the enrollee while in an emergency or crisis situation and a scheduled appointment was not possible due to the need of an immediate response" ([§ 62A.673, subd. 2(h)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). Verified against the Revisor's live text on July 29, 2026.

> ⚠ re-check: this clause, twice over.
>
> First, the date. On July 1, 2027, audio-only drops out of the commercial definition of telehealth unless the legislature acts again. The 2027 date was set in the 2025 First Special Session, which amended subdivision 2 ([Laws 2025, 1st Spec. Sess. ch. 3, art. 8, s. 1](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/laws/2025/1/Session+Law/Chapter/3/)); that section is unconditionally effective, and the Minnesota House's summary of the same law says "Access to audio-only telehealth will be extended through June 30, 2027" ([house.mn.gov](https://www.house.mn.gov/NewLaws/story/2025/5634)) — the same window said two ways.
>
> Second, a contingency this draft previously flagged only on the Medicaid side. The published text of subdivision 2 carries a "[See Note.]" marker, and the note reads: "The amendment to subdivision 2 by Laws 2022, chapter 98, article 4, section 1, is effective upon federal approval. The commissioner of human services shall notify the revisor of statutes when federal approval is obtained" ([Revisor note to § 62A.673](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673), confirmed live July 29, 2026). No published approval date was found. That note attaches to the 2022 amendment, not to the 2025 date change, so the 2027 sunset itself is not in question — but a piece whose whole thesis is date-and-contingency discipline should say that subdivision 2 has an unresolved federal-approval note on its face.
>
> ⚠ CONFIRM: whether any 2026 or 2027 bill extends the audio-only definition again. Do not write that another extension is expected. Nothing in this draft supports that.

There is a wrinkle worth flagging for anyone reading the statute themselves. A separate, standalone audio-only coverage *mandate* used to live in subdivision 6(b), and it ended years ago: that paragraph closes with "This paragraph expires July 1, 2023" ([subd. 6(b)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62A.673)). Audio-only survives today only because it is written into the *definition* of telehealth, which is the clause that sunsets in 2027.

## If you are on Medical Assistance or MinnesotaCare

Minnesota Medicaid has its own parity rule: "Medical assistance covers medically necessary services and consultations delivered by a health care provider through telehealth in the same manner as if the service or consultation was delivered through in-person contact. Services or consultations delivered through telehealth shall be paid at the full allowable rate" ([Minn. Stat. § 256B.0625, subd. 3b(a)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625)). Mental health has its own hook: "Subject to federal approval, mental health services that are otherwise covered by medical assistance as direct face-to-face services may be provided via telehealth in accordance with subdivision 3b" ([subd. 46](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625)).

MinnesotaCare follows Medical Assistance here, because "covered health services" are defined as "the health services reimbursed under chapter 256B," with a list of exceptions — special education, home care nursing, nonemergency medical transportation, personal care assistance and case management, community first services and supports, behavioral health home services, housing stabilization services, and nursing home or intermediate care facility services — that does not include telehealth ([Minn. Stat. § 256L.03, subd. 1(a)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256L.03)).

Audio-only on the public-program side is a window, not a permanent rule. The Medicaid definition excludes audio-only "except that from July 1, 2025, to July 1, 2027, telehealth includes communication between a health care provider and a patient that solely consists of audio-only communication" ([§ 256B.0625, subd. 3b(e)(1)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625)). Unlike the commercial clause, that sentence attaches no scheduled-appointment or standard-of-care condition on its face.

> ⚠ re-check + ⚠ CONFIRM: the 2025 amendment creating that window is "effective upon federal approval," and the Revisor's note still instructs the commissioner of human services to "notify the revisor of statutes when federal approval is obtained" ([Revisor note to § 256B.0625](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625), confirmed live July 29, 2026). No published approval date was found. The same contingency language appears in subd. 46. Meanwhile the DHS provider manual appears to operate as though audio-only is covered — but see the verification note below; this draft could not read that page. That tension is unresolved here and should be reported to readers as an open question, not smoothed over.

Documentation is a condition of payment on the Medicaid side: "As a condition of payment, a licensed health care provider must document each occurrence of a health service delivered through telehealth to a medical assistance enrollee," with required elements including the type of service, start and end times, "the health care provider's basis for determining that telehealth is an appropriate and effective means for delivering the service to the enrollee," the mode of transmission, and the location of the originating and distant sites ([§ 256B.0625, subd. 3b(c)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625)). Minnesota Health Care Programs providers attest to those same practices on a signed form, the "Telehealth Provider Assurance Statement," which states that "Telehealth services are granted to eligible providers enrolled with MHCP" ([DHS-6806-ENG, rev. 1-24](https://edocs.dhs.state.mn.us/lfserver/Public/DHS-6806-ENG)).

Two more Medicaid-specific points. The Medicaid telehealth provider list is broader than the commercial one, adding "a community paramedic," "a community health worker," "a mental health certified peer specialist," "a mental health certified family peer specialist," "a mental health rehabilitation worker," and "a mental health behavioral aide" ([subd. 3b(e)(2)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625)). And for the clinics where a great deal of Minnesota Medicaid therapy is delivered, the statute is permissive and conditional, not automatic: "Telehealth visits provided through audio and visual communication or accessible video-based platforms **may be used to satisfy** the face-to-face requirement for reimbursement under the payment methods that apply to a federally qualified health center, rural health clinic, Indian health service, 638 tribal clinic, and certified community behavioral health clinic, **if the service would have otherwise qualified for payment if performed in person**" ([subd. 3b(d)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625); emphasis added). Note also that this paragraph says audio *and* visual. It is not an audio-only allowance.

## What Minnesota's own review found

The legislature asked for study, and the Minnesota Department of Health delivered a final report dated September 16, 2024. Its first two recommendations were to keep parity — the first one with a limiting clause that matters:

> "Recommendation 1: Payment parity should continue for real-time (synchronous) audio-visual and audio-only telehealth for health care services for which telehealth may substitute for, and is comparable to, in-person care. If evidence emerges that there are significant or meaningful cost savings without sacrificing quality or satisfaction, the payment structure could be revisited."
>
> "Recommendation 2: Audio-only telehealth should continue to be included in the definition of telehealth in Minnesota statute, and therefore be subject to payment parity and coverage requirements."

MDH names behavioral health first among the reasons for Recommendation 2: audio-only "has filled an important gap in health care availability and access, particularly for people seeking behavioral health care, older Minnesotans, those with complex chronic conditions, and those residing in areas with low broadband access" ([MDH telehealth final report, 2024](https://www.health.state.mn.us/data/economics/telehealth/docs/telefinalreport24.pdf)).

On whether telehealth is worse care, MDH's synthesis is measured: "to date, telehealth has expanded access to health care without appearing to compromise health care quality or patient satisfaction" (same report). That is the state's reading of the literature, not a promise about any individual course of therapy.

Mental health is where telehealth landed hardest. MDH reports that "Over 50% of mental and behavioral health visits were conducted via telehealth in 2022," and that among commercial enrollees, "19% of primary care visits and 26% of behavioral health visits were delivered via telehealth in 2022, compared to 3% and 2%, respectively, in 2019." On the supply side, as of July 2023 "about 75% of behavioral health providers[] report using telehealth for at least some of their visits" — a provider self-report survey figure, not a count of Minnesota practitioners. Audio-only volume looks small in claims data: "less than 2% of all commercial enrollees in the MN APCD had an audio-only visit in 2022," versus 5% of Medicare Advantage enrollees. MDH cautions that those two figures "only represent claims for services that were paid by insurance and were specifically coded as audio-only and are likely an undercount of audio-only visits" (all from the [MDH report](https://www.health.state.mn.us/data/economics/telehealth/docs/telefinalreport24.pdf)).

DHS made the equity case for audio-only to legislators in March 2025: "An evaluation of audio-only telehealth using data from the U.S. Census Bureau's Health Pulse Survey found that almost 50% of people of color used audio-only to access telehealth, compared with 38% of white patients" ([DHS 2025 session priority sheet, 03/2025](https://mn.gov/dhs/assets/2025-03-audio-only-telehealth-hc-54_tcm1053-669211.pdf)). The same sheet is the plainest statement of what was at stake: without action, "Medical Assistance and MinnesotaCare enrollees who get essential mental and physical health care services by phone will lose that access on July 1, 2025."

One caution on citing that research: the MDH study covered private-sector health insurance, and DHS ran a separate parallel study of Minnesota Health Care Programs ([MDH telehealth page](https://www.health.state.mn.us/data/economics/telehealth/index.html)). MDH's commercial-market numbers should not be presented as describing Medicaid enrollees.

## Why this article insists on dates

Because Minnesota's own web copy lags its own statute. As of this drafting, MDH's telehealth landing page still tells readers that during the 2023 session the legislature "passed a law that extends coverage for audio-only telehealth care until June 30, 2025," and carries "Last Updated: 09/23/2024" ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/data/economics/telehealth/index.html), confirmed live July 29, 2026). That page is not wrong about the 2023 law. It is simply older than the 2025 change.

## Reasonable questions to ask

When calling a plan or a practice, these are answerable questions:

- Is this plan a Minnesota-issued insured plan, a self-funded employer plan, Medical Assistance, MinnesotaCare, or Medicare-related? That answer determines which rules above apply.
- What is my cost share for a telehealth therapy visit, and is it the same as in person?
- Is the therapist I want in network for telehealth, not only for office visits?
- If video is not workable, is a phone session covered, and under what conditions?
- Does anything about this require prior authorization that an in-person session would not?
- If the plan says a telehealth session is not covered, is the reason medical necessity, a plan exclusion, or a medical management rule — and what is the appeal process?
