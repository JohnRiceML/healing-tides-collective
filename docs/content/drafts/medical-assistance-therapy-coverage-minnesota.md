# Does Medical Assistance or MinnesotaCare cover therapy?

Short answer: yes, both do — and under both programs there is no cost-sharing for mental health services. The harder part is usually not whether therapy is a covered benefit. It is finding a therapist who is enrolled, accepting new patients, and able to see you this month.

This article walks through what the law actually says, because the gap between "covered" and "available" is where most people get stuck.

## What Medical Assistance covers

Medical Assistance is Minnesota's Medicaid program. Psychotherapy is covered outright, with one condition attached to who delivers it: [Minn. Stat. § 256B.0671](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0671) provides that "Medical assistance covers psychotherapy if a mental health professional or a clinical trainee provides psychotherapy to a client."

For a concrete sense of what the program means by outpatient mental health care, [Minn. Stat. § 256B.0625](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625) sets the floor for a community mental health center: at minimum, "diagnostic assessment; explanation of findings; family, group, and individual psychotherapy, including crisis intervention psychotherapy services, psychological testing, and medication management."

Telehealth is not a lesser benefit. Under the same section, MA "covers medically necessary services and consultations delivered by a health care provider through telehealth in the same manner as if the service or consultation was delivered through in-person contact," and those services "shall be paid at the full allowable rate." Subdivision 46 addresses mental health telehealth specifically, subject to federal approval. If no one within driving distance takes MA, this is the practical door.

## What MinnesotaCare covers

MinnesotaCare does not maintain a separate benefit list. [Minn. Stat. § 256L.03](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256L.03) defines covered services as "the health services reimbursed under chapter 256B" — the MA statute — minus a specific set of carve-outs: special education services, home care nursing, nonemergency medical transportation, personal care assistance and case management, community first services and supports, behavioral health home services, housing stabilization services, and nursing home or ICF services.

Outpatient psychotherapy is not on that carve-out list, so it rides along with MA coverage. Worth noticing, though: several *wraparound* mental health supports are carved out. Case management, behavioral health home services, and housing stabilization are MA benefits that MinnesotaCare does not cover. Therapy transfers; the scaffolding around it does not always.

## Which program you fall into

The two programs stack by income.

Medical Assistance for adults without children reaches up to 133 percent of the federal poverty guidelines ([§ 256B.056](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.056)), with that group defined as adults at least 21 and under 65 who are not pregnant and not on Medicare ([§ 256B.055, subd. 15](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.055)).

MinnesotaCare is the band above it, and it is **not** limited to families with children. [Section 256L.04](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256L.04) covers both groups in the same income band: subdivision 1 covers "[f]amilies with children with family income above 133 percent of the federal poverty guidelines and equal to or less than 200 percent of the federal poverty guidelines for the applicable family size," and subdivision 7 separately covers "all individuals and families with no children who have incomes that are above 133 percent and equal to or less than 200 percent of the federal poverty guidelines for the applicable family size." If you are a single adult over the MA limit, MinnesotaCare is still your program.

Two narrower paths also exist in the same section: children under 19 at or below 200 percent who are ineligible for MA solely because of federal household-composition rules (subd. 1b), and people ineligible for MA based on immigration status with income at or below 200 percent (subd. 10(b)).

## What it costs — the part most people are surprised by

**Medical Assistance: no cost-sharing.** The relevant section is titled, in the statute itself, "PROHIBITION ON COST-SHARING." It provides that "the medical assistance benefit plan must not include cost-sharing or deductibles for any medical assistance recipient or benefit" ([§ 256B.0631](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0631)). A managed-care plan's own 2026 benefit grid says the same thing in plain terms: "There is no Medical Assistance cost-sharing in 2025 or 2026" ([Medica MHCP benefits grid](https://partner.medica.com/-/media/documents/provider/programs-and-resources/mhcp-overview-of-benefits-grid.pdf?la=en&hash=E3414C17A2209F0E74DAF66B837A8DAB)).

⚠ **re-check — this is changing, but not soon.** The 2026 Legislature amended § 256B.0631 to carve MA expansion adults (those eligible under § 256B.055, subd. 15) out of the cost-sharing prohibition, and struck the words "Effective January 1, 2024" from that subdivision ([Laws 2026, ch. 127](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/laws/2026/0/Session+Law/Chapter/127/)). The new cost-sharing applies only to expansion adults with income above 100 percent of FPL and only "for services provided on or after October 1, 2028." Two protections matter for therapy: the new copays are "subject to the exceptions and limits required by Public Law 119-21, section 71120," and the federal provision behind them caps cost-sharing at $35 while excluding "mental health services, substance use disorder services, services provided by a Federally qualified health center …, certified community behavioral health clinic …, or rural health clinic" ([CRS R48633](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48633/R48633.1.pdf)). Separately, the Minnesota text provides that "Providers must not deny services to recipients who are unable to pay the co-payment or deductible," and that the state's payment to the provider is reduced by the copay amount.

⚠ CONFIRM (statutory reading, needs Nora's and/or a billing reviewer's sign-off): the new $3 charge is "$3 per nonpreventive visit," where a visit is defined as an episode of service "delivered in an ambulatory setting by a physician or physician assistant, chiropractor, podiatrist, nurse midwife, advanced practice nurse, audiologist, optician, or optometrist." Psychologists, clinical social workers, LMFTs and LPCCs are not on that list. On the face of the text, a therapy hour is not a copay-triggering visit. This is a reading of statutory language, not DHS guidance, and it should not be stated as settled in the published version.

⚠ CONFIRM: whether CMS has approved Minnesota's 2026 cost-sharing amendment. Describe it as contingent until confirmed.

**MinnesotaCare: premiums and copays — but not for mental health.** DHS's eligibility manual states it flatly. Adults 21 or older have a monthly deductible plus copays for non-preventive visits, nonemergency ER visits, eyeglasses and prescriptions — and then: "There is no cost sharing for mental health services" ([DHS Health Care Programs Manual 3.1.2.2](https://hcopub.dhs.state.mn.us/epm/3_1_2_2.htm)). Both carriers whose 2026 documents are public say the same. Medica lists the office-visit copay as "$28 (No copay for mental health visits)" and puts "Mental Health services (to include antipsychotic drugs)" and "Substance Use Disorder treatment" on its no-copay list. UCare's member page describes "$28 copay for an office visit that isn't for preventive care or mental health and substance use disorder services" ([UCare MinnesotaCare](https://www.ucare.org/health-plans/medicaid/64-under/minnesotacare)).

⚠ re-check annually — those are 2026 plan-year amounts and each carrier sets its own within the DHS schedule.

Note where the mental health carve-out comes from. [Section 256L.03](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256L.03) itself exempts only children under 21, American Indians as defined in federal regulation, and — a recent addition — mobile crisis intervention and crisis assessment. The general mental health exemption lives in the benefit schedule the commissioner designs to hold the plan at 94 percent actuarial value, not in the statute. That is a meaningful distinction: schedules are easier to change than statutes.

⚠ CONFIRM: the dollar amount of the MinnesotaCare monthly deductible. DHS's manual confirms adults 21+ have one but states no figure, and no reachable official source gave one. Do not publish a number.

**Premiums.** For the 2026 plan year, MinnesotaCare premiums run from $0 to $80 per person per month on a sliding scale, with $0 for households below 35 percent of the federal poverty guideline, everyone under 21, American Indians and Alaska Natives and their family members, and recent military members ([DHS-4139A, effective Jan. 1–Dec. 31, 2026](https://edocs.dhs.state.mn.us/lfserver/Public/DHS-4139A-ENG)). The scale is set in statute, per person 21 or older, from $4 at 35 percent FPG up to $80 at 200 percent ([§ 256L.15](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256L.15)). ⚠ re-check each January when the new premium table posts.

One caution about the copay protection: it is one visit deep. DHS says "Providers must serve MinnesotaCare enrollees who are not able to pay a copay or deductible at the time of the visit, however, that provider does not have to serve an enrollee again if their cost sharing is still not paid."

## The diagnostic assessment is the real gate

This is the piece almost nobody knows going in, and it shapes the first several weeks of care.

Under [Minn. Stat. § 245I.10, subd. 2(a)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/245I.10), "[a] license holder must use a client's diagnostic assessment or crisis assessment to determine a client's eligibility for mental health services, except as provided in this section." The exceptions are the rest of that subdivision. Before an initial diagnostic assessment is complete, a short list of services can be delivered — including "any combination of psychotherapy sessions, family psychotherapy sessions, and family psychoeducation sessions not to exceed three sessions."

The assessment has to land somewhere. It must "identify at least one mental health diagnosis for which the client meets the diagnostic criteria," or "include a finding that the client does not meet the criteria for a mental health disorder." Coverage is diagnosis-driven, not distress-driven.

A **brief** diagnostic assessment supports more sessions, but the allowance is conditional, and the condition is the part that gets dropped in summaries. Subdivision 2(d) reads in full: "Based on the client's needs in the client's brief diagnostic assessment, a license holder may provide a client with any combination of psychotherapy sessions, group psychotherapy sessions, family psychotherapy sessions, and family psychoeducation sessions not to exceed ten sessions within a 12-month period without prior authorization for any new client or for an existing client who the license holder projects will need fewer than ten sessions during the next 12 months." So the ten-session pathway is open to a new client, or to an existing client the clinician expects to finish inside ten sessions. An existing client expected to need ten or more is on the standard-assessment track instead.

A **standard** diagnostic assessment is required when the client needs "services of a greater number or intensity" than the pathways above allow, when the client needs more services and does not meet the criteria for a brief assessment, when the condition "has changed markedly," when the current condition no longer meets the criteria of the current diagnosis, or "upon the client's request" (subd. 2(f)). It must include "a face-to-face interview with a client and a written evaluation of the client," and it is substantially longer — the statute lists current living situation, basic needs, medications, immediate risks, treatment history, cultural influences and more.

One clarification, because it is easy to get backwards: the credential requirement is the same for both. "Only a mental health professional or clinical trainee may complete a brief diagnostic assessment of a client" (subd. 5(a)), and the identical restriction applies to the standard assessment (subd. 6(a)). What differs is scope and what triggers each one, not who may sign it. Usefully, subd. 5(d) allows a clinician to use the sessions a brief assessment authorized to gather the additional information needed for a standard assessment when the number of sessions will exceed the subdivision 2 limits — so the brief-to-standard handoff does not have to mean a gap in care.

If you read an older article that cites Minnesota Rules part 9505.0371 for any of this, it is out of date. That rule now reads simply: "[Repealed, L 2021 c 30 art 17 s 113]" ([Minn. R. 9505.0371](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/9505.0371/)). The requirements moved into chapter 245I.

⚠ CONFIRM: whether MA fee-for-service imposes any annual session cap beyond the pre-assessment limits above. No such cap was located, but neither was an affirmative official statement that none exists. Do not write "unlimited sessions."

## The honest reason it's hard to find someone

Coverage is the easy half. Here is the mechanism behind the waitlists, stated in the state's own documents.

**The rate.** DHS's January 2024 rate study found that for services delivered by mental health professionals, "the multiplier is $28.44, or 83.9% of the Medicare rate," and explained why: "due to a lack of legislative budget authority to increase payment rates overall, DHS is forced to 'back into' state-specific multipliers each year to ensure that total spending remains budget neutral" ([DHS rate study](https://mn.gov/dhs/assets/Final%20Rate%20Study%20Report_tcm1053-610638.pdf)). The report recommends moving to 100 percent of Medicare.

**A second discount on top — on a provision scheduled to disappear.** [Section 256B.0625, subd. 38](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0625) provides that payments for mental health services delivered by masters-prepared mental health professionals "shall be 80 percent of the rate paid to doctoral-prepared professionals," except for those employed by community mental health centers, who are paid 100 percent. The differential applies to the masters-level licenses on the "mental health professional" list — licensed independent clinical social workers, marriage and family therapists, and licensed professional clinical counselors — rather than to licensed psychologists or psychiatrists. "Mental health professional" is a closed list of six independently licensed roles under [§ 245I.04, subd. 2](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/245I.04), which is also why a pre-licensure therapist can only bill as a supervised clinical trainee.

⚠ **Important, and easy to miss:** the Revisor's page carries "[See Note.]" under subdivision 38, and that note reads: "Subdivision 38 is repealed by Laws 2025, First Special Session chapter 3, article 8, section 43, effective on the latest of the following: (1) federal approval of the repeal of this subdivision; (2) federal approval of the amendment to Minnesota Statutes, section 256B.76, subdivision 6 …; (3) federal approval of the amendments to Minnesota Statutes, section 256B.761 …; or (4) federal approval of all necessary federal waivers to implement the managed care organization assessment in Minnesota Statutes, section 256B.1976." Trigger (3) is the same federal approval the market-based-rate paragraph below turns on. So the 80/100 split is current law today and conditionally repealed on the event that would raise the underlying rates. Do not build advice on it. ⚠ re-check whether the repeal has taken effect before publishing or updating this section.

**A legislated fix, not yet confirmed as live.** [Section 256B.761](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.761) requires the commissioner to "establish and pay market-based payment rates" for psychotherapy and other behavioral health services effective for services rendered on or after January 1, 2026, "or on or after the date of federal approval, whichever is later." Those rates "must not be lower than: (i) the payment rates recommended in the rate analysis required by Laws 2021, First Special Session chapter 7, article 17, section 18, and published by the Department of Human Services on January 22, 2024; or (ii) the payment rates in effect on December 31, 2025." Managed care and county-based purchasing plans "must reimburse providers at an amount that is at least equal to the fee-for-service rate," and the commissioner "must monitor the effect of this rate adjustment on enrollee access." The section does not apply to federally qualified health centers, rural health centers, Indian health services, or certified community behavioral health clinics, or to cost-based or county-negotiated rates (subd. 4). ⚠ re-check — this is the single most important thing on this page to revisit, and ⚠ CONFIRM whether CMS has granted the federal approval the statute conditions it on.

**Some participation is conditioned on taking MA.** Under [§ 256B.0644](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.0644), a vendor of medical care must participate in MA and MinnesotaCare as a condition of participating in state employee plans, public employee plans, workers' compensation and MCHA — where participation means the provider "accepts new medical assistance and MinnesotaCare patients" or has "at least 20 percent of the provider's patients" covered by those programs. (What the Legislature's reasons were is not stated in the section, and no legislative history is cited here.)

**And enrolling is not a form.** A therapist cannot simply bill MA. Under [§ 256B.04, subd. 21](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.04), DHS screens and enrolls providers, "a provider must enroll each provider-controlled location where direct services are provided," and each provider must be revalidated "at least once every five years."

**What the state's own barrier survey actually says.** DHS's FY23-24 behavioral health legislative report asked Minnesota's 19 Adult Mental Health Initiative regions to list the gaps and barriers to services in their FY2023/2024 funding applications. In the resulting "Top Barrier to Receiving Services, 2024" table, the barriers named by the most regions were "Access to transportation" (13 regions, 68 percent) and "Lack of housing" (12, 63 percent), then a tie between "Lack of culturally specific programs/services/providers" and "Workforce shortage" (10 each, 53 percent), then "Long wait times" (9, 47 percent), then a tie between "Cost of services" and "Lack of Psychiatric and IRTS beds" (7 each, 37 percent) ([DHS report](https://mn.gov/dhs/assets/BHD-Mental-Health%20-Legislative-Report_FY23-24_tcm1053-698609.pdf)). Read the whole list rather than the middle of it: workforce and wait times are real constraints, but the two things regions named most often were getting there and having somewhere to live. ⚠ re-check when the next biennial report publishes.

⚠ CONFIRM — deliberately absent: no reachable official source reports the share or number of Minnesota therapists who accept MA or MinnesotaCare, and none quantifies the average wait for an MA-covered appointment. Do not add a percentage or a wait time. The widely repeated "70 of 87 counties are mental health shortage areas" line traces to a 2016 MDH document that now 404s; it is not usable.

MDH's 2025 Health Access Survey found the rise in uninsurance "appears to be driven in part by a decrease in public coverage (e.g., Medical Assistance, MinnesotaCare, Medicare, Tricare), which dropped from 44.1% to 39.6%" ([MDH 2025](https://www.health.state.mn.us/data/economics/hasurvey/docs/mnha2025.pdf)). ⚠ re-check with the next survey release.

## Where the door is most likely to be open

None of the following guarantees an appointment. These are the places where the structure of the law makes an open door more likely.

**Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics.** Certification is voluntary — [§ 245.735](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/245.735) frames its requirements as things "[e]ntities that choose to be CCBHCs must" do — but the requirements a clinic takes on when it certifies are unusually access-oriented. A CCBHC must "ensure that clinic services are available and accessible to individuals and families of all ages and genders with access on evenings and weekends and that crisis management services are available 24 hours per day," and must "establish fees for clinic services for individuals who are not enrolled in medical assistance using a sliding fee scale that ensures that services to patients are not denied or limited due to an individual's inability to pay for services." Note the limits of that second one: the sliding-fee duty is written for people *not* enrolled in MA, and nothing in the section obliges a CCBHC to accept a particular MA enrollee. Evening, weekend and 24-hour crisis coverage is the part that matters most if your schedule or your timeline is the problem. CCBHC services are also among the categories the federal cost-sharing provision excludes.

**Community mental health centers.** § 256B.0625, subd. 5(e) sets a statutory minimum service list for a CMHC — diagnostic assessment, explanation of findings, individual, group and family psychotherapy, crisis intervention psychotherapy, psychological testing and medication management — so a CMHC is, by definition, staffed to do the assessment and the therapy in one place rather than referring the assessment out. (There is also a payment-rate reason CMHCs have historically been better positioned to take MA, but as noted above, that provision is conditionally repealed and should not be relied on.)

**Telehealth, and looking outside your county.** MA pays telehealth at the full allowable rate. If your county has no one, the search radius is the whole state.

**Ask the plan, not just the directory.** A note on the limits of network-adequacy law: [§ 62K.10](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/62K.10) does require networks to include "a sufficient number and type of providers, including providers that specialize in mental health and substance use disorder services … without unreasonable delay" — but chapter 62K "applies only to health plans offered in the individual market or the small group market" (§ 62K.02, subd. 2(a)), so it governs commercial carriers, not MA managed care. It is not the lever an MA enrollee can pull.
